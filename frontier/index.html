Linear-time matrix transposition on multitape Turing machines · statement (T)

Transposition Impossibility Frontier

The rectangle is every deterministic multitape Turing machine that transposes a d × d matrix of ⌈log2 d⌉-bit entries (read-only input, k work tapes, write-only output). Circles are machine properties and overlap as in a Venn diagram. Each piece is coloured by what is known about transposing in time O(L), L = d2⌈log2 d⌉. The four red bands cut across every circle: a one-tape machine, for instance, may or may not be oblivious. Hover or tap any piece for its status and sources.

The pieces

How the colours are decided

Theorem D of the fourth paper splits impossibility for oblivious machines into two statements. Depth reduction (TCP): a genie revealing εL bits cuts the machine's time–space dependency graph to bounded depth. The crux: index codes for the side information of the remaining bounded-depth cones need many bits. Paper 14 proves depth reduction for every number of heads, so for oblivious machines only the crux remains. Directed Recovery Structures proposed a machine-free sufficient condition for the crux, Question 9.1: rate at most ½ for every storage code on recovery relations of bounded degree with paired-distance separation. Paper 13 answers it negatively. The coding hypotheses on the map concern relations arising from tape computations, including the frozen traces of a fixed simulator. Paper 15 now constructs abstract codes with polynomially small rate deficits, ruling out the unrestricted slow-gap premise that was previously another sufficient condition. It does not settle the native coding hypotheses.

Red means an unconditional exclusion. The red circle combines depth reduction with proved half-rate bounds for its cone relations (papers 7, 8 and 10). Orange needs the coding crux at a rate above the reveal cost. Dark orange needs it only at the smaller depths of Theorems F, B and M. Yellow uses a stronger coding hypothesis, a half-rate bound for native recovery relations. In the low-adaptivity circle Corollary L(b) uses the original machine’s frozen traces. In the outer yellow region Paper 15 applies that corollary to a simulator, at the simulator’s own resource bounds (Corollary 9.3). Paper 16 replaces half rate by a fixed native deficit (Theorem G). The new abstract construction does not give these native relations, so the yellow regions retain their colour.

Paper 9 also gives a route that needs no coding question. For an oblivious machine whose head excursions overlap boundedly, complete source certificates on the actual return geometry show directly that the machine is wrong on most inputs, provided only a few excursions are large. That is the small red island inside the dark orange piece.

Paper 11 adds three things. Given the crux for ball systems, hop charging excludes every oblivious transposer whose code merges deep data lineages only boundedly often, on every schedule however deeply nested (the dashed island; for linear codes the linear crux suffices). Before paper 14 this was the only route to deep nesting; now it changes the hypothesis, not the colour, and paper 11's Conjecture HV*, code-level depth reduction, holds (paper 14, Corollary HV). Given a half-rate bound for the relations of its coarse-skeleton classes, bounded entry on every tape, the input tape included, is excluded at any adaptivity (Corollary RV, the yellow disc), and a machine whose work tapes have bounded entry must carry about L/2 bits of the data in the coarse motion of its input head (Corollary IS). Paper 11 states these results under Question 9.1, but its proofs use it only for the machine's own relations, inside balls and of skeleton classes, and for hop charging the crux for ball systems serves as well. Unconditionally, paper 9's exclusions extend to machines of sufficiently low adaptivity (Theorem LA), and every linear-time transposer, adaptive or not, runs an external-memory transposition with O(d) I/Os that is oblivious on 2L−O(d) inputs (Theorem EM), so an I/O lower bound without the indivisibility assumption would refute (T).

Paper 13 answers Question 9.1 negatively. Twisted Goren–Oort Tanner codes give recovery relations of bounded, though astronomically large, degree on which every paired distance tends to infinity, with storage codes of rate above 0.5021, and binary ones above 0.5013; the guaranteed rates approach 4−2√3 ≈ 0.536. Forgetting directions, it also answers the fourth paper's undirected crux (its Question 6.1) negatively. Its relations are not known to arise from tape machines, and its codes are not shown to have rate near 1 by that construction alone. Paper 15 uses its odd-field linear codes as seeds for a majority amplification that does reach rates approaching one as the degree cap grows. A half-rate impossibility proof must use the structure of tape computations, not only bounded degree and paired distances. The same is now true of the slow-gap route near rate 1. Expansion, a two-class twisted structure and algebraic local codes supply the seed; the amplified codes still have no known tape realization. Expanding dependency structures are compatible with tape geometry (papers 4 and 8), but that compatibility does not provide the required computation.

Paper 14 proves the trajectory cut problem for every number of heads: the walk DAG of any k walks, with or without pauses, has a cut of at most εT edges after which no path has more than D(k,ε) long hops, where log log D(k,ε) = O(k² log(k/ε)) for ε ≤ ½ (Theorem T, Lean). The deep-nesting pairs, the series' sharpest candidates for depth-robust schedules, are cut to depth D(2,ε) whatever their nesting depth (Corollary N, Lean), so Conjectures A′ and A″ are false. Every trace of every machine therefore has a genie of cost εL and bounded full depth, and the crux alone excludes oblivious linear-time transposers, as the linear crux does for linear ones (Corollary O, Lean in the frozen machine model). The same genies give the ball systems of these machines index codes of εL bits at a bounded radius, so paper 11's Conjecture HV* holds (Corollary HV, Lean). This removed the yellow piece of earlier versions of the map, oblivious deep nesting under two open statements, and turned the whole half-bound circle red. Conversely, an oblivious transposer needs, for every ε, storage codes of rate at least 1 − ε on its own cone relations at the depth of Corollary O. Corollary L(b) excludes a machine whose trace entropy is at most ηL for a fixed η < ½, given the native half-rate bound for its frozen traces (Lean). At any fixed block scale, L(a) also gives a statement about the original machine. Under the native bound at the specified depth, every linear-time transposer’s coarse skeleton has entropy above (½ − δ)L for each fixed δ > 0 at all sufficiently large dimensions.

Paper 15 proves in Lean that any linear-time transposer can be simulated by a fixed linear-time machine whose trace has entropy at most L/4 at every sufficiently large canonical length. Its larger finite control is fixed before the dimension grows. If the native half-rate bound holds for each such simulator at its own value width and depth, Corollary L(b) would require entropy above L/4. This gives the outer adaptive region its yellow colour (Corollary 9.3). The native coding hypothesis remains open. A linear-time transposer forces binary storage codes whose rates approach one, on relations of incidence degree at most Δ in which every paired distance tends to infinity: at least 1 − K·(log log log Δ / log log Δ)1/(4k²+1) at every large cap Δ, where K depends only on the machine and its time bound (Theorem A, Lean). The paper now constructs abstract binary families with the stronger rate bound 1 − CΔ−α, for fixed C > 0 and 0 < α < ½ (Theorems M and C). Each sufficiently large cap Δ is fixed before the code length and minimum paired distance tend to infinity. At that cap, one common partition at every sufficiently large length works for every nonempty source subset, retains every coordinate, and has an exponential tail for the loss in fibre rate (Theorem F). Thus the unrestricted slow-gap premise of Corollary 4.1 is false (Corollary 6.2); even the machine-forced abstract rate witnesses exist without a machine. These amplification, counting and padding results are proved in Lean from an explicit seed hypothesis. A written proof supplies the seed from Paper 13; the upstream arithmetic geometry is not formalized here. The partitions supply no tape realization or bound on its physical access cost.

On the simulator's own bit positions, recovered at their actual append times, Paper 15's typical cells have the same rate-loss exponent 1/(4k²+1) as the packet codes (Theorem N, Lean). Revealing whole batches and counting their joint record support gives this stronger bound. The codes retain at least (1 − 2√(log₂ Δ)/Δ)L original bit positions. On any nonempty timely successful set G, one partition works for every u ≥ 0: the fraction of inputs whose cell rate is below log₂|G|/L − A·(log log log Δ / log log Δ)1/(4k²+1) − u is at most 2−uL. Codes whose rates approach one cannot recover most bits using patches of bounded target count and overlap (Theorem P and Corollary 10.3). These statements constrain native realizations; the native coding hypotheses remain open, so no region changes colour.

Paper 16 works on the native question directly, and its main results are proved in Lean. Its main theorem is a necessary condition: every oblivious linear-time transposer must route a linear amount of information through macroscopic crossings. Two revisits on one tape cross when one begins inside the other and ends after it, as when a stretch of tape is written moving right and later read moving right again, like a queue; reuse like a stack makes only nested revisits. For a fixed fraction θ, the macroscopic crossing steps are the starts of revisits that travel more than θL away and cross another such revisit on the same tape. From every set of at least αL outputs, any set of steps that cuts all dependency paths from those outputs to the macroscopic crossing steps has at least a fixed fraction as many elements as there are outputs, with θ and the fraction explicit in k, the alphabet and state counts, the time constant and α (Theorem X). Stack-like reuse at macroscopic scale is therefore excluded, whatever the code. At scale θL/4 the head walks of a linear-time run come from a finite family of coarse shapes, independent of d, and one coarsely monotone leg of one tape carries the expansion (Theorem S). The same holds on every trace of a linear-time transposer that is followed by at least a 2−ηL fraction of the inputs, for an explicit tiny η, and on the heavy traces of paper 15’s simulator of any linear-time transposer, which carry all but an exponentially small fraction of the inputs; carrying the conclusion back to the original machine’s own dependency graph is open (Theorem E). A set of steps that cuts the reads of some input rows from the outputs they send to some output rows must carry all of that data, whatever the coding (Lemma R), but one set of steps is such a cut for every pair of row sets at once, so counting cuts alone cannot go further. These necessary conditions do not settle the orange pieces: five explicit linear-time cascade schedules, realized by machines that compute other functions, meet all of them in numerical tests. Two of these exclusions are drawn as red islands at the lower left of the oblivious circle: few macroscopic crossings (Theorems X and S) and one long-range work head (Theorem H). Each meets both oblivious pieces, and since both theorems hold on every heavy trace of any machine, each also reaches into the low- and high-adaptivity regions. No existing piece turns red as a whole.

Paper 16 also adds a red band, the one below the sweeps band: no machine, adaptive or not, transposes in linear time if on every run its output is produced in a final stretch in which the input head and a fixed set of bank heads make O(1) monotone phases, up to o(L) jitter, every other head stays within o(L) cells, and a fixed fraction of the input rows is still unread. Its proof gives a depth-independent coding deficit on the cone relations of paper 15’s simulator. With the whole run as the stretch, the class probably also yields to paper 1’s column-grouped pass bound once the jitter and the workspace are folded into the finite control (a proof sketch); with an unrestricted prefix the published pass bounds say nothing. Paper 16 also weakens the yellow hypotheses: a fixed native deficit, rate at most 1 − ρ(k, C) for some ρ(k, C) > 0, uniformly over k-tape machines with time constant C and at every depth, refutes (T) (Theorem G), and its proof shows that under low adaptivity ρ above the trace-entropy rate suffices; half rate is the case ρ = ½. It localizes Corollary O to one machine at a time. It also excludes, unconditionally, every oblivious or low-adaptivity machine whose work heads have bounded nesting load and family heights in a fixed number of dyadic windows of fixed width, whatever the input head does: a pigeonhole over scales leaves a gap that no family occupies, which puts the machine in paper 9’s island. It also shows, unconditionally, that an oblivious or low-adaptivity linear-time transposer needs at least two work heads that each return to a cell after moving a fixed fraction of L away: one arbitrary work head, with every other work head confined to smaller excursions, is excluded whatever the input head does, because a slab partition of the free head’s tape cuts the computation into nearly autonomous pieces (Theorem H). Outside the red band, a transposer must use unboundedly many macro-phases on its large tapes, or linear workspace, while a fixed fraction of the input is unread.

A negative answer to (T) for all machines would give the first superlinear lower bound for integer multiplication on multitape machines (Harvey and van der Hoeven). The best known upper bound is O(L·(log d)1−κ), from an unrefereed preprint.
