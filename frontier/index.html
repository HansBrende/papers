Linear-time matrix transposition on multitape Turing machines · statement (T)

Transposition Impossibility Frontier

The rectangle is every deterministic multitape Turing machine that transposes a d × d matrix of ⌈log2 d⌉-bit entries (read-only input, k work tapes, write-only output). Circles are machine properties and overlap as in a Venn diagram. Each piece is coloured by what is known about transposing in time O(L), L = d2⌈log2 d⌉. The three red bands cut across every circle: a one-tape machine, for instance, may or may not be oblivious. Hover or tap any piece for its status and sources.

The pieces

How the colours are decided

Theorem D of the fourth paper splits impossibility for oblivious machines into two statements. Depth reduction (TCP): a genie revealing εL bits cuts the machine's time–space dependency graph to bounded depth. The crux: index codes for the side information of the remaining bounded-depth cones need many bits. Paper 14 proves depth reduction for every number of heads, so for oblivious machines only the crux remains. Directed Recovery Structures proposed a machine-free sufficient condition for the crux, Question 9.1: rate at most ½ for every storage code on recovery relations of bounded degree with paired-distance separation. Paper 13 answers it negatively. The coding hypotheses on the map concern relations arising from tape computations, including the frozen traces of a fixed simulator, with one exception: paper 15's machine-free rate gap, which bounds the rates on all such relations, though only by 1 − ρ(Δ) instead of ½.

Red means an unconditional exclusion. The red circle combines depth reduction with proved half-rate bounds for its cone relations (papers 7, 8 and 10). Orange needs the coding crux at a rate above the reveal cost. Dark orange needs it only at the smaller depths of Theorems F, B and M. Yellow uses a stronger coding hypothesis, a half-rate bound for native recovery relations. In the low-adaptivity circle Corollary L(b) uses the original machine’s frozen traces. In the outer yellow region Paper 15 applies that corollary to a simulator, at the simulator’s own resource bounds. Paper 15 also adds a second yellow hypothesis, which excludes every machine at once: the machine-free rate gap. Like the native bound it is stronger than the crux, since paid trimming turns a short index code for the cones into a high-rate code on a relation of bounded degree with growing paired distances (paper 14, Remark 9.5). Neither of the two yellow hypotheses implies the other.

Paper 9 adds a third route that needs no coding question. For an oblivious machine whose head excursions overlap boundedly, complete source certificates on the actual return geometry show directly that the machine is wrong on most inputs, provided only a few excursions are large. That is the small red island inside the dark orange piece.

Paper 11 adds three things. Given the crux for ball systems, hop charging excludes every oblivious transposer whose code merges deep data lineages only boundedly often, on every schedule however deeply nested (the dashed island; for linear codes the linear crux suffices). Before paper 14 this was the only route to deep nesting; now it changes the hypothesis, not the colour, and paper 11's Conjecture HV*, code-level depth reduction, holds (paper 14, Corollary HV). Given a half-rate bound for the relations of its coarse-skeleton classes, bounded entry on every tape, the input tape included, is excluded at any adaptivity (Corollary RV, the yellow disc), and a machine whose work tapes have bounded entry must carry about L/2 bits of the data in the coarse motion of its input head (Corollary IS). Paper 11 states these results under Question 9.1, but its proofs use it only for the machine's own relations, inside balls and of skeleton classes, and for hop charging the crux for ball systems serves as well. Unconditionally, paper 9's exclusions extend to machines of sufficiently low adaptivity (Theorem LA), and every linear-time transposer, adaptive or not, runs an external-memory transposition with O(d) I/Os that is oblivious on 2L−O(d) inputs (Theorem EM), so an I/O lower bound without the indivisibility assumption would refute (T).

Paper 13 answers Question 9.1 negatively. Twisted Goren–Oort Tanner codes give recovery relations of bounded, though astronomically large, degree on which every paired distance tends to infinity, with storage codes of rate above 0.5021, and binary ones above 0.5013; the guaranteed rates approach 4−2√3 ≈ 0.536. Forgetting directions, it also answers the fourth paper's undirected crux (its Question 6.1) negatively. Its relations are not known to arise from tape machines, and its codes are not shown to have rate near 1, which a transposer needs (paper 15, Theorem A), so no piece changes colour. What changes is the evidence. A half-rate impossibility proof must use the structure of tape computations, not only bounded degree and paired distances, so a half-rate bound on a machine's own relations now stands on its own (hence the yellow); a machine-free proof remains possible only near rate 1 (paper 15); and a construction now knows one way to beat rate ½: expansion, a two-class twisted structure and algebraic local codes. Expanding dependency structures are compatible with tape geometry (papers 4 and 8), but rate near 1 would need a different mechanism.

Paper 14 proves the trajectory cut problem for every number of heads: the walk DAG of any k walks, with or without pauses, has a cut of at most εT edges after which no path has more than D(k,ε) long hops, where log log D(k,ε) = O(k² log(k/ε)) for ε ≤ ½ (Theorem T, Lean). The deep-nesting pairs, the series' sharpest candidates for depth-robust schedules, are cut to depth D(2,ε) whatever their nesting depth (Corollary N, Lean), so Conjectures A′ and A″ are false. Every trace of every machine therefore has a genie of cost εL and bounded full depth, and the crux alone excludes oblivious linear-time transposers, as the linear crux does for linear ones (Corollary O, Lean in the frozen machine model). The same genies give the ball systems of these machines index codes of εL bits at a bounded radius, so paper 11's Conjecture HV* holds (Corollary HV, Lean). This removed the yellow piece of earlier versions of the map, oblivious deep nesting under two open statements, and turned the whole half-bound circle red. Conversely, an oblivious transposer needs, for every ε, storage codes of rate at least 1 − ε on its own cone relations at the depth of Corollary O. Corollary L(b) excludes a machine whose trace entropy is at most ηL for a fixed η < ½, given the native half-rate bound for its frozen traces (Lean). At any fixed block scale, L(a) also gives a statement about the original machine. Under the native bound at the specified depth, every linear-time transposer’s coarse skeleton has entropy above (½ − δ)L for each fixed δ > 0 at all sufficiently large dimensions.

Paper 15 proves in Lean that any linear-time transposer can be simulated by a fixed linear-time machine whose trace has entropy at most L/4 at every sufficiently large canonical length. Its larger finite control is fixed before the dimension grows. If the native half-rate bound holds for each such simulator at its own value width and depth, Corollary L(b) would require entropy above L/4. This gives the outer adaptive region its yellow colour. The coding hypothesis remains open. The paper's second route needs no machine. A linear-time transposer forces binary storage codes whose rates approach one, on relations of incidence degree at most Δ in which every paired distance tends to infinity: at least 1 − K·(log log log Δ / log log Δ)1/(4k²+1) at every large cap Δ, where K depends only on the machine and its time bound (Theorem A, Lean). Conversely, if all such codes have rate at most 1 − ρ(Δ), where ρ(Δ)·(log log Δ)a is unbounded for every a > 0 (any constant gap will do, and so will ρ(Δ) ≥ c/log log log Δ), then no machine transposes in linear time (Corollary 4.1 and its all-tapes form, Lean); paper 14 had sketched this for oblivious machines (Remark 9.5). Known constructions guarantee rates of only about 0.536 (paper 13), and the elementary gap 1/(Δ+1) is far too small, so the question is open in both directions. On the simulator's own bit positions the typical cells of inputs are codes of rate near one as well (Theorem N), and such codes cannot recover most of their bits inside boundedly overlapping patches (Theorem P).

A negative answer to (T) for all machines would give the first superlinear lower bound for integer multiplication on multitape machines (Harvey and van der Hoeven). The best known upper bound is O(L·(log d)1−κ), from an unrefereed preprint.
